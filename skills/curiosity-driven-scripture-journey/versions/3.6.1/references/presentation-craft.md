# Presentation craft for Scripture learning

Use when planning or revising a spoken study, YouTube script, narrated experience, teaching visuals, or live presentation. For a website or printed study, apply the writing, evidence, and visual-sequencing sections; performance directions are unnecessary unless someone will speak the work. A narrow task loads only the relevant section. Research IDs P1–P13 live in `presentation-evidence.md`. The practical techniques below are craft judgments informed by that evidence, not individually proven formulas.

## Plan what each moment accomplishes

Inherit the audience, toward goal, genuine away concern, learning targets, and source trail from the Journey Concept. Identify the learner's likely question in ordinary words. Clarify what the content should help someone understand, remember, and optionally do, and what emotional tone fits the truth of the material. These are connected planning questions, not a mandatory feel-first order.

For substantial spoken content, extend the existing outline with compact delivery notes where choices matter:

| Beat / learner question | Point, evidence, and limit | Spoken treatment | Visual treatment and transition | Learner action / later return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What is being resolved here? | What can actually be supported? | Wording, emphasis, pace, pause, or tone that clarifies it | Face, source, diagram, example; why and when it changes | Notice, compare, explain, optional recall, or apply |

Do not fill every cell for every sentence. The purpose is to make important choices recordable and executable, without turning the lesson into a rigid sequence. Actual footage selection, transcript cleanup, retry removal, source timecodes, and video assembly remain in independent production/transcript projects.

## Open with a useful foothold

Choose an opening that fits the question: a recognizable situation, a sourced scene, a visible discrepancy, a concrete question, or a short demonstration. Establish enough context for a new learner to understand the gap. Name the kind of answer the content can deliver and start giving evidence promptly.

- **Question opening:** put two short, relevant statements where they can be compared and name the apparent difference.
- **Scene opening:** begin near the meaningful action; introduce only the context needed to understand it. Use the PAST and source rules in `story-craft.md`.
- **Demonstration opening:** show a reading or reasoning problem, invite a low-pressure prediction, then explain what the evidence changes.
- **Direct-answer opening:** give a useful answer immediately when urgency, complexity, or the learner's task calls for it; build interest through why the answer holds and where it applies.

An opening does not have to shock, accuse, insult, or begin negatively. Courtesy can be brief and natural. A video description/title may reserve an honest reveal; the teaching itself delivers understanding throughout. Do not fill the opening with authority claims or a long promise of later value.

## Write for the ear and for thought

Use familiar words with precise meanings. Define a necessary unfamiliar term beside its first use; repeat its stable label later. Put the actor and action near each other. Break a sentence when it contains several qualifications the listener must hold before reaching the point. Preserve necessary complexity by sequencing it.

Vary sentence length deliberately. A longer sentence may carry a scene or explanation; a short sentence can land a distinction. Avoid giving every sentence the same punchy rhythm. Repetition, parallel phrasing, and a small set of steps may make structure audible, but no rhetorical count is a biological memory limit.

Make the relationship between sections audible:

| Relationship | Useful transition |
|---|---|
| New evidence changes an initial reading | State the initial reading, then the specific evidence that complicates it. |
| A conclusion follows | State the reason before using “therefore”; check that the inference follows. |
| Two cases differ | Name the exact dimension of comparison, then keep it stable. |
| A prerequisite is missing | Explain what must be known before continuing. |
| Time advances without a causal claim | Use a chronological transition; sequence alone does not establish causation. |
| A question is resolved | State the answer and why the next question now matters. |

The videos' contrast-and-consequence transitions can be useful craft. Do not force conflict into every paragraph, mechanically insert “but/therefore,” or turn a historical sequence into an invented causal chain. Optional callbacks reconnect a familiar scene or phrase to a new piece of evidence. They should add a relationship, correction, retrieval opportunity, or application.

Conversational address has experimental support in particular multimedia lessons (P4), but it does not require artificial intimacy or assumptions about the learner. Use “you” where natural. Preserve the skill's restriction on an unnamed teacher “we/us/our.”

## Make a scene teach

Select details because they make an action, relationship, consequence, or setting understandable. A scene need not mention every sense. Give the learner a clear orientation, a relevant action, and a change that matters. A precise verb can do more than several decorative adjectives.

After the scene, explicitly connect its relevant feature to the passage or question. Explain where the comparison stops. If the main idea can be stated only by retelling the entire anecdote, strengthen the meaning handoff. If the memorable detail directs attention to a different subject, revise it (P5). A story may carry emotion and still need a diagram, direct explanation, or comparison.

Use illustrations to make evidence inspectable. Do not present invented dialogue, thoughts, weather, gestures, or sensory detail as biblical/history facts. Clearly labeled hypothetical examples remain examples. Learning the scene and learning its intended principle are separate outcomes; build the recall and transfer opportunities from `memory-and-learning.md`.

## Voice, emphasis, rhythm, and silence

**Phrase by meaning.** Read aloud during drafting. Put a breath or pause where a thought closes, where a comparison changes sides, or where a listener needs to inspect evidence. Avoid cutting a qualifier away from the claim it limits.

**Emphasize the distinction.** Mark the few words that change the meaning. Contrast can be expressed through stress, a small pitch change, a pause, or a visual cue. Emphasizing everything removes hierarchy. When reading Scripture, keep wording accurate and distinguish interpretive emphasis from the quoted text.

**Vary pace with the task.** Familiar orientation can move briskly; new names, dense evidence, and comparisons need room. Speech-rate findings depend on the task and audience (P8–P9). Do not prescribe one ideal words-per-minute target or accelerate speech to weaken scrutiny. Account for second-language listeners, caption reading, hearing needs, and the opportunity to replay.

**Vary vocal energy with meaning.** Curiosity can sound inquisitive; a difficult consequence may call for steadiness; a resolution may sound relieved or clear. Use natural pitch, volume, and articulation without requiring a particular accent, personality, pitch range, or theatrical intensity. Clear consonants and audible sentence endings help intelligibility. Conviction cannot substitute for evidence, and hesitation or a speech difference is not evidence of dishonesty.

**Give pauses a job:**

- orientation: establish the next question or source;
- reading: let the audience locate and inspect a quotation or graphic;
- processing: let a difficult relationship settle before adding another;
- participation: allow a prediction, explanation, or recall attempt, with an option to pause the video;
- emotional recovery: allow an authentic moment to land before moving into explanation.

Choose duration through the actual reading/response task and rehearsal. Avoid a fixed dramatic-pause quota. Preserve meaningful silence when planning the edit; a thought or reading pause is not automatically dead air. Brief natural disfluency need not be polished away at the cost of warmth or meaning.

## Face, gesture, and physical presentation

Use the face when expression, testimony, a sincere question, or an explanation of significance adds information. Look toward the lens for direct address where comfortable; look toward an object or display when directing attention there. Eye contact can serve a social or attentional purpose, but instructor-visibility findings do not justify maximizing face time (P6–P7). Do not prescribe prolonged staring or infer engagement from whether a learner meets a gaze.

Let gestures point to a location, show scale, contrast two positions, trace a sequence, or demonstrate an action. Keep a repeated concept in a consistent spatial location. Stop moving when stillness lets the evidence be read. Avoid meaningless looping gestures or movement that covers important labels. Preserve natural delivery and accommodate physical differences.

For a live room, check the microphone, sightlines, screen readability, and the speaker's position relative to evidence. Face the audience while speaking when feasible; point or move with a clear purpose. Repeat an audience question before answering when others may not have heard it. A calm response that acknowledges uncertainty can be stronger teaching than a rehearsed display of certainty.

## Choose shots and visuals by their job

| Current task | Useful treatment | Transition cue |
|---|---|---|
| Pose a human question or land a personal meaning | Presenter face with uncluttered framing | Move when the explanation needs visible evidence. |
| Inspect a verse or historical source | Readable excerpt, source label, enough surrounding context | Hold through the relevant reading and reasoning; highlight only what is being discussed. |
| Compare two claims | Stable side-by-side layout with consistent labels | Keep both available until the comparison is explained. |
| Explain a relationship or sequence | Build a diagram as each relation is named | Add one meaningful relation at a time, then show the integrated whole. |
| Demonstrate a physical process | Hands/object/process view | Preserve the action needed to understand the result. |
| Convey setting or illustrate an application | Relevant B-roll or a labeled illustration | Return when it has completed its explanatory job. |
| Invite recall | A short prompt with necessary context but without the answer | Allow an attempt, then show and explain the answer. |
| Synthesize or invite a next step | Face, completed diagram, or concise summary | Finish the answer before presenting a voluntary continuation. |

**Face → evidence → face example:** hold a genuine expression through the completion of the thought; switch as the referenced text or visual becomes the object of attention; return to the face when the speaker explains significance or addresses the learner again. Do not require a cut on every expression change or on a timer. Static frame samples can show composition, but only playback supports claims about exact transition timing.

For teaching slides, consider a concise message heading supported by the evidence needed to evaluate it. Label interpretation explicitly. A source excerpt, diagram, comparison, or timeline can be the evidence; it need not be a photograph. One study of technical slides supports this combined approach, with limits (P10). Avoid walls of text, but preserve accurate quotations and necessary qualifications. Never enlarge a statistic or draw a persuasive chart before its claim has been checked.

Guide attention through proximity, a pointer, a restrained highlight, or progressive construction. Keep labels next to their referents and synchronize the explanation. Leave completed context visible when it remains necessary. Dynamic drawing may help in some settings (P6–P7); it is not a reason to animate every word.

Use B-roll to establish, explain, document, or illustrate. Distinguish documentary footage from reenactment or illustrative stock when that distinction affects truth. Show faces, graphics, and text at sizes that serve the learner's task. A picture-in-picture speaker is optional, especially when space is needed for evidence.

Keep speech intelligible over music and effects. Use sound to support context or a meaningful transition; avoid a constant emotional crescendo or suspense bed that implies danger the evidence does not establish. Check whether the reasoning and tone remain fair without music. Retain accurate captions and accessible equivalents. A visual cue should not carry the only explanation of a distinction.

## Rehearse for comprehension

Build rehearsal into a substantial spoken-content handoff as a practical production step, scaled to the assignment. If only a script is requested, provide the plan and cue difficult passages; do not claim rehearsal occurred.

1. **Read for meaning:** speak the script naturally; simplify where syntax forces recovery. Check names, quotations, transitions, and whether the opening's promise is fulfilled.
2. **Listen without the screen:** can a learner follow the argument, evidence references, and comparisons? Add verbal orientation where sight alone carried the reasoning.
3. **Inspect the visuals:** can the key text be read at intended viewing size? Do highlights and builds correspond to the spoken point? Keep labels/source citations legible.
4. **Invite a fresh explanation, if a reviewer is available:** ask what the person understood, which evidence supports it, and where the thread became unclear. “Was it engaging?” alone is insufficient.
5. **Revise the specific difficulty:** adjust wording, information order, visual support, pace, or pause; preserve natural personality. Practice the opening, complex evidence transitions, and ending in context.

A shorter recording or smaller practice audience can make preparation manageable. Notes and scripts are legitimate supports; authenticity does not require improvising. Do not promise to eliminate fear, diagnose anxiety, or treat a self-talk exercise as brain reprogramming.

## End with understanding the learner can use

Resolve the governing question explicitly, name the supporting evidence and any important limit, and connect an optional callback to that explanation. Use a brief recall or transfer invitation when useful, followed by a model answer or review route. A next step should be specific, voluntary, and accurately described. Ending on a strong feeling alone does not establish learning.

Review using the artifact's existing quality gate plus `ethical-persuasion.md`. Keep audience interest, comprehension, delayed recall, transfer, and voluntary action distinct. No presentation recipe guarantees any of them.
