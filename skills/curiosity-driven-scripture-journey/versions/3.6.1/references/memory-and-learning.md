## Core planning contract

Every substantial teaching artifact names what the learner should understand, what should remain retrievable, and what the learner should be able to do with it. Put the plan in the existing Journey Concept or script outline. Scale detail to the task; a short piece may need one compact row. Do not create a second project record.

Use this planning row for each central learning target:

| Target and source | Prior knowledge / likely confusion | Meaningful chunk and representation | Learner processing | Recall and explanatory feedback | Later return / transfer |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The supported distinction, relationship, or practice to retain | What must be defined or repaired first | What is introduced together and what stays visible | Notice, compare, explain, predict, trace, or apply | A prompt, expected answer/rationale, and help route | Where it returns with a useful change of wording or context |

Name one governing learning outcome and a manageable set of supporting targets. The number depends on complexity, audience knowledge, duration, and available practice. Do not convert a working-memory estimate into a fixed number of lesson objectives. A narrow styling or title task can inherit the existing plan without rebuilding it.

## What memory terms mean for design

These are useful distinctions across interacting systems, not a literal conveyor belt through isolated brain boxes (W1).

| Term | Working explanation | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory registration and attention | Seeing or hearing something supplies rapidly changing input; selective attention determines what receives further processing. | Make the relevant thing perceptible and identifiable. Exposure alone is not learning. |
| Short-term retention | Information remains briefly available without becoming durable knowledge. Continued support and interference affect availability. | Keep a term, verse, comparison, or instruction visible while it is needed. Avoid a universal seconds-to-forgetting claim. |
| Working memory | Holding relevant information while comparing, interpreting, or using it, with limited attention and processing resources. | Reduce simultaneous unfamiliar relationships and interface demands. Show the material needed for the current reasoning step. |
| Long-term knowledge and memory | Information can remain available beyond the current task and support later interpretation and action. Access depends partly on cues and what was learned. | Build meaningful connections, practice retrieval, revisit after delays, and test use in another context. |

Distinguish remembering an event or example (episodic), understanding a meaning or relationship (semantic), and becoming able to carry out a practiced procedure. A vivid story can be remembered while its lesson is missed. A learner recognizing a verse on screen may still be unable to explain its context. Specify which outcome matters.

### The brain-system explanation, with limits

The hippocampus and connected medial temporal structures contribute to forming and binding many new declarative memories. Distributed cortical systems also participate; memory organization changes over time through consolidation. These accounts have unresolved questions, especially about enduring episodic detail (W5). Working-memory models emphasize maintained information plus attention/control, rather than a single anatomical storage box (W1).

For production, this supports giving new material context, organizing it meaningfully, and planning opportunities to use it again. It does not establish a particular lesson length, an exact transfer time into long-term memory, or a guarantee that an emotional moment is permanently stored. The actionable retrieval and spacing guidance comes from behavioral learning evidence (L1–L2).

### How many items can working memory handle?

Under conditions limiting rehearsal and grouping, research has often suggested roughly **3–5 unfamiliar chunks**, centered near four (W2–W3). A chunk is a meaningful unit to the learner: an expert's familiar phrase or pattern may contain several elements a beginner must manage separately. Visual-memory findings also support resource and precision tradeoffs, so capacity is not simply a universal count of slots (W4).

Use the number as a load warning, not a ceiling on visible cards, navigation items, sentences, or total study length. The older “7 plus or minus 2” slogan is not a universal design allowance either. Difficulty depends on what must be held and related at once, not just how many things appear on screen.

### A practical load pass

- Inventory the unfamiliar names, terms, passages, chronology, and relationships required at each step. Teach prerequisites before asking for a complex comparison.
- Give each explanatory unit a dominant purpose. If several new relationships compete, build the parts first and then show the integrated whole.
- Keep relevant evidence visible together; label it next to the point of use. Avoid sending a reader through several disclosures just to compare two claims.
- Externalize support with a small map, glossary, timeline, stable passage labels, or a short completed-example explanation. Keep optional detail available without making it another prerequisite.
- Reuse terminology consistently. When a synonym matters, identify the relation explicitly.
- Let the learner pause, replay, revisit, or inspect a simpler example. Adapt to observed confusion and prior knowledge; do not diagnose a capacity or learning style from a click.
- Preserve purposeful beauty and motion while removing competition at demanding moments. A difficult passage can receive a still, readable hold before motion resumes at a meaningful transition.

## Build memory opportunities throughout the work

### Before new material: orient and activate

Name the useful question and connect it with a familiar passage, experience, or concept. Offer a brief prerequisite explanation where needed. A prediction can expose a knowledge gap and invite attention, provided the learner has enough context and receives an explanation afterward (C2). Avoid a misleading pre-question whose incorrect answer is left standing.

### During explanation: give meaning and structure

Show the passage or source, identify the relevant observation, and explain the inference in plain language. Use an example, comparison, timeline, or diagram when it makes the relationship easier to inspect. Ask the learner to explain a connection in their own words where useful (L3); provide a model answer that distinguishes text, interpretation, and application.

For beginners, demonstrate a reasoning move before asking for an independent version. For experienced learners, allow the worked support to be skipped. Teach the process of reading context and evaluating evidence, as well as the conclusion.

### After attention has moved: retrieve and correct

Return to an important idea after a meaningful intervening step. Invite the learner to recall, explain, reconstruct, or choose with a reason, then supply feedback grounded in the source. Feedback should say why an answer works, identify a likely confusion, and offer a short route back to the evidence. Recalling before reviewing can support later memory (L1). A copied sentence or an already-visible answer is not evidence of unaided recall.

Use `LP-FM-04` when later accessibility of knowledge matters. Learner participation stays voluntary: give “review first,” “show explanation,” or a print equivalent, and never gate the core teaching behind a correct response. If the objective is source use, allow lookup and evaluate interpretation instead of pretending to test memorization. Assess understanding of the passage and argument; never score faith, agreement, spiritual worth, or personal disclosure.

### At the end: synthesize and transfer

Land the promised answer clearly. Invite a brief explanation of the central relationship and its source, followed by a new example, passage-comparison question, or scenario that calls for the same reasoning. Reveal a sourced model response and acknowledge legitimate interpretive uncertainty. A closing recap helps orientation; an attempt to reconstruct the idea gives a different opportunity to learn.

### Later: spaced return

Design an optional review card, printable prompt, bookmarkable revisit, or a retrieval question at the opening of the next episode. Return to the important idea with a different cue or application and feedback. For an initial pilot, a later section, the next day or two, and a later week are practical options. Timing must fit the intended retention period and actual learner performance (L2); these intervals are not an optimal universal schedule. Do not create reminders or send follow-ups without the user's request.

Vary a return purposefully: first explain the distinction, later find its evidence, later apply it in another context. Preserve stable core terminology while changing the task. Do not impose a repetition quota, repeat claims until they feel true, or use a slogan as a substitute for reasoning.

## Multimedia, pacing, and memory

Coordinate narration, text, and visual evidence around the same current relationship. More channels do not imply unlimited capacity (W3). Detailed visual information needs time to inspect. Explanatory labels should appear when and where their referents are discussed. A spoken sentence, a long unrelated text block, and moving decoration create competing tasks.

Keep accurate captions and accessible transcripts available. Do not remove accessibility text because of an oversimplified redundancy rule. Use readable phrase grouping, sufficient contrast and duration, and controls where supported. When reading Scripture aloud, preserve the wording and source label; visually indicate the current phrase without changing the verse to fit an animation.

For video, segment at changes in concept, evidence, scene, or learner job. Learner-paced segmentation has experimental support in multimedia tasks (V1), but no source here establishes a universal cut interval or human attention span. Give enough time to read a graphic and understand the point before changing it. Return to the presenter's face for a purposeful conversational or emotional beat; use source text, illustration, or B-roll when it conveys relevant evidence. Expression changes alone do not prove improved retention.

A question during a video may create useful retrieval and reorientation; attention/learning results vary across studies (V2–V3). Use a natural pause or invitation to pause, then a clear answer. Scale the number to the learning target and format. Shorts may support one compact idea; a longer study can revisit several connected targets. Runtime follows the evidence and the audience's task.

## A cross-format example

Suppose the learning target is distinguishing a passage's wording from a teacher's interpretation. Supply a verified passage and a clearly labeled interpretation; preserve their source status throughout.

| Stage | Website | Printed or facilitated study | Video |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notice | Adjacent text and interpretation with clear labels | Two labeled excerpts | Hold the two labeled statements as the speaker explains |
| Process | Optional selection of the words supporting the inference | Underline evidence and explain the link | Invite a brief prediction; then identify the evidence |
| Retrieve | After another section, ask for the distinction with an optional answer reveal | Close the page briefly or use a separate prompt | Ask for the distinction before displaying the recap |
| Transfer | Apply the distinction to a new sourced example | Discuss a new example using the same process | Show a second example and explain the reasoning |
| Revisit | Bookmarkable review with source links | A review card for another session | Next episode begins with a brief recall and explanation |

These are design examples, not mandatory widgets or experimental findings.

## Review and research methods

Before delivery, inspect whether the key idea can be followed with the evidence visible, whether every planned recall has a correct and sourced explanation, whether later returns perform a new job, and whether all core material is accessible without participation. Check curiosity resolution and motive framing alongside memory opportunities.

When evaluating with learners, choose the target and scoring criteria before inspecting results. A practical pilot can use:

1. A short prior-knowledge question to distinguish a missing prerequisite from an unsuccessful presentation.
2. Separate interest/relevance and friction feedback, alongside completion or viewing behavior when available.
3. An immediate explanation and evidence-identification question.
4. Delayed recall and a new transfer example after a stated interval, with the same rubric across variants.

Ask what the passage supports and what remains uncertain. Preserve disagreement as a legitimate response. Use anonymous or minimal data where possible, and do not infer religious commitment or diagnose cognitive traits from performance.

For a causal comparison, vary one feature (for example question-before-explanation versus explanation-only), randomly assign comparable learners when feasible, keep content and opportunity to learn comparable, and include a delayed outcome. Report exposure/time differences, prior knowledge, completion, attrition, sample size, and uncertainty. A small convenience pilot is exploratory. A repeated quiz can itself affect later scores; keep assessment exposure comparable or use parallel prompts. Do not claim measured learning gains from an expert design review or a higher click-through rate (V4).
