## How to use the evidence

- **Experiment:** supports the tested comparison, population, task, and outcome. Transfer to a Scripture Journey remains an application to evaluate.
- **Observational study:** supports an association; avoid a causal promise.
- **Theory/review:** explains a model or body of findings; identify it as such.
- **Practitioner framework:** useful for generating questions; it is not a validated classification of people.
- **Design judgment:** the skill's proposed implementation, not an experimentally established duration, count, or script formula.

Keep the source, finding, limitation, and intended use together. Research dates are publication dates, not web crawl dates. An abstract-only inspection supports only what the abstract reports. No numerical effectiveness claim should be added without checking the original methods and outcome.

## Motivation

**M1 — The remembered four-part matrix.** Jim Taylor, [Business: The Drive to Succeed](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-power-of-prime/201005/business-the-drive-to-succeed), 2010. Original practitioner account of internal/external × positive/negative motivation. Use only to identify the user's remembered terminology. The article is not a validation study of four personality types; its broad success/happiness predictions are not adopted. The skill's toward/away prompts are a related design adaptation, not interchangeable scientific scales.

**M2 — Approach/avoidance and planning.** Bø et al., [Working for the Best or Bracing for the Worst?](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/7/1/21173/116442/Working-for-the-Best-or-Bracing-for-the-Worst), 2021. Two preregistered experiments, total N = 1,349, did not find a significant framing effect on willingness to plan. This does not establish equivalence in every context; it rules out presenting approach motivation as proof that someone is organized. Judge framing by relevance and learning outcomes.

**M3 — Support for autonomous motivation.** Deci, Eghrari, Patrick, and Leone, [Facilitating Internalization: The Self-Determination Theory Perspective](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/1994_DeciEghrariPatLeone.pdf), 1994. Experimental support for providing a rationale, acknowledging feelings, and conveying choice during an uninteresting activity. Classroom and ministry applications require contextual judgment. Use meaningful reasons and voluntary participation; do not treat compliance as internal conviction.

**M4 — Motivation definitions.** Ryan and Deci, [Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation from a self-determination theory perspective](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/2020_RyanDeci_IntrinsicandExtrinsic.pdf), 2020. Authoritative theory/review distinguishes intrinsic interest from different forms of extrinsic regulation, including autonomous forms. Internal/external language in a popular matrix is not the same construct. Autonomy, competence, and relatedness inform the skill's respectful choices, scaffolding, and belonging.

**M5 — Framing can change the experience of a task.** Sakaki et al., [Motivated with joy or anxiety](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13415-024-01154-3), 2024. In a stopwatch-task/fMRI study, approach framing produced more positive reported emotion, while avoidance framing produced more anxiety/disappointment. Reward-network cue activity was similar across frames, with other representational differences. This supports considering the learner's experience, not a simple happy-brain/fear-brain division or a guarantee of better retention. It does not cancel M2: emotional experience and willingness to plan are different outcomes.

**M6 — Loss aversion has a specific research context.** Kahneman and Tversky, [Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf), 1979. Choice experiments and a descriptive model address gains/losses relative to a reference point and decisions under risk. This is not evidence that all learning or religious motivation is fear of loss, or that a loss-framed hook always works better. Use genuine consequences accurately, without turning the model into an audience diagnosis.

## Curiosity, dopamine, and emotion

**C1 — Curiosity and later memory.** Gruber, Gelman, and Ranganath, [States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25284006/), 2014. Trivia/fMRI experiments found better memory for high-curiosity answers and some incidental material, including after a day; anticipatory activity and connectivity implicated reward and memory regions. fMRI did not directly measure dopamine release or prove a content technique causes a chemical response. Supports testing relevant questions and satisfying answers, not claims about engineered dopamine spikes.

**C2 — Prediction can stimulate curiosity.** Brod and Breitwieser, [Lighting the wick in the candle of learning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31646002/), 2019. In a numerical-facts experiment, making a prediction elicited more high-curiosity ratings than generating an example; higher curiosity was associated with better answer memory. Pupillary measures are not a dopamine assay. Adapt as an optional, answerable prediction followed by timely evidence and correction; do not assume every prediction task improves learning.

**C3 — Reward prediction and dopamine.** Schultz, Dayan, and Montague, [A neural substrate of prediction and reward](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9054347/), 1997. Primate physiological research and computational analysis linked dopamine-neuron activity to reward prediction errors. It does not establish a video editing schedule or prove that surprise, pleasure, curiosity, and dopamine are the same thing. Use only as qualified background. No variable-reward or compulsion technique follows from this skill.

**C4 — Emotion is selective.** Waring and Kensinger, [How emotion leads to selective memory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21414333/), 2011. In emotional-scene research, memory for salient items could improve at the expense of background context. An emotional story can therefore be memorable while its supporting reasoning is missed. The design implication is to reconnect the scene to the passage and evidence, not increase arousal.

## Memory systems and capacity

**W1 — Definitions and models.** Cowan, [What are the differences between long-term, short-term, and working memory?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18394484/), 2008. Researcher review of competing definitions: short-term retention, working-memory maintenance/processing, and enduring knowledge interact. Use the distinctions as explanatory models, not literal serial boxes or a fixed countdown to forgetting.

**W2 — Approximate chunk capacity.** Cowan, [The magical number 4 in short-term memory](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf), 2001. Theoretical synthesis of evidence controlling rehearsal and grouping argued for roughly four chunks in the focus of attention. A chunk depends on prior knowledge; the estimate is neither a universal biological limit nor a rule that pages can contain only four visible items.

**W3 — Cross-modal load.** Saults and Cowan, [A central capacity limit to the simultaneous storage of visual and auditory arrays in working memory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17999578/), 2007. Experiments supported a central limitation alongside modality-specific storage under the tested conditions. Adding a second sensory channel does not provide unlimited capacity. Coordinate words and visuals and reduce the information that must be held and compared simultaneously.

**W4 — Capacity and precision.** Bays and Husain, [Dynamic shifts of limited working memory resources in human vision](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Acd1bee82-d2f7-4247-86fd-7c77f17e0131), 2008. Visual-memory experiments supported flexible allocation of limited resources and a tradeoff in precision. Competing models make an exact universal item count inappropriate. Complexity and the precision a task requires also matter.

**W5 — Brain systems and consolidation.** Squire, Genzel, Wixted, and Morris, [Memory consolidation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26238360/), 2015. Researcher review describes hippocampal/neocortical contributions and changes in memory organization over time, while acknowledging competing accounts. It supports a cautious systems explanation, not a claim that every remembered item moves from a short-term box into one permanent storage location. No educational schedule can be read directly from this neuroscience.

## Practices for durable learning

**L1 — Retrieval and delayed retention.** Roediger and Karpicke, [Test-enhanced learning](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/), 2006. Controlled prose-learning experiments found delayed retention advantages from retrieval over additional study, while immediate performance and confidence could favor restudy. A familiar-looking answer is not evidence that a learner can later recall or explain it. Adapt with low-pressure recall and explanatory feedback; exact effects depend on the task.

**L2 — Spacing.** Cepeda et al., [Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/), 2008. More than 1,350 participants learned facts with review gaps and later tests extending over months. Useful spacing depended on the desired retention interval; more delay was not always better. The skill's later-section, next-day, and later-week examples are practical starting points, not universal optimal schedules.

**L3 — Generative explanation.** Chi et al., [Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding](https://www.public.asu.edu/~mtchi/papers/Self-explanations94.pdf), 1994. Prompting explanations while learning a science text improved understanding in that setting. Adapt by asking learners to explain how textual evidence supports an interpretation, then provide a model explanation. An unsupported guess or theological agreement is not a measure of understanding.

## Multimedia and sustained attention

**V1 — Learner-paced segments.** Mayer and Chandler, [When learning is just a click away](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Mayer_Chandler01.pdf), 2001. Experiments with narrated animation supported learner-controlled segmentation for transfer. Applies most directly to processing explanatory sequences, not to arbitrary cuts or universal clip lengths. Use conceptual boundaries, readable holds, and replay/continue controls.

**V2 — Questions during a video.** Szpunar, Khan, and Schacter, [Interpolated memory tests reduce mind wandering and improve learning of online lectures](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1221764110), 2013. Two small experiments with a 21-minute statistics lecture found attention and learning benefits from intervening tests. This is evidence for a possible useful intervention, not a guaranteed attention reset every few minutes.

**V3 — A boundary on that result.** [Interpolated testing and content pretesting as interventions to reduce task-unrelated thoughts during a video lecture](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35348931/), 2022. A later study found a small reduction in task-unrelated thought from interpolated testing without supporting Bayes-factor evidence, and no statistical evidence of a learning benefit or matched-content pretesting benefit. Do not equate more quizzes with more learning; test the actual treatment and audience.

**V4 — Watching behavior.** Guo, Kim, and Rubin, [How video production affects student engagement](https://juhokim.com/files/LAS2014-Engagement.pdf), 2014. Observational analysis of 6.9 million MOOC watching sessions related production features to watch duration and assessment attempts. Confounding and platform context limit causal claims. Engagement metrics can locate friction; they do not establish durable learning or an ideal length for all videos.

## Updating this ledger

For a new claim, record the population/task, method, measured outcome, delay, comparator, access level, limitations, and exact proposed use. Prefer original papers and author/institutional copies; use scientific reviews to explain models and locate primary evidence. Seek contradictory or null results before strengthening a rule. Keep laboratory findings, design inferences, and results from actual Scripture Journey users visibly distinct.
