## Persuasion and belief

**P1 — Relevance, argument quality, and source expertise.** Petty, Cacioppo, and Goldman (1981), [Personal involvement as a determinant of argument-based persuasion](https://richardepetty.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/1981-jpsp-pettycacioppogoldman.pdf), DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.41.5.847. Original author abstract inspected; scanned author-hosted paper located. Experiment with 145 undergraduates concerning proposed senior examinations. Personal relevance, argument quality, and source expertise were manipulated. Under high relevance, attitudes depended more on argument quality; under low relevance, source expertise had more influence. Immediate attitude outcome, not a universal emotion-versus-logic rule or learning trial. **Use:** establish relevance and make reasons inspectable. Do not use inattention as an opportunity to replace evidence with status.

**P2 — Narrative absorption and belief.** Green and Brock (2000), [The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives](https://weatherhypepodcast.com/uploads/1/0/8/8/10887248/the_role_of_transportation_in_the_persuasiveness_of_public_narratives.pdf), DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701. Original paper inspected. Four experiments studied story absorption, belief, and character evaluation; processing instructions manipulated transportation in one experiment. Greater absorption related to story-consistent beliefs; factual/fictional labeling generally did not remove the effects. These outcomes do not establish better comprehension or long-term retention. **Use:** meaningful narrative followed by explicit evidence and limits; do not design for reduced scrutiny.

**P3 — Limits of the 7/38/55 formula.** Albert Mehrabian, [author's clarification of Silent Messages](https://www.kaaj.com/psych/smorder.html). First-person explanation of the research's scope, inspected in full. The equation concerns communication of feelings/attitudes, especially inconsistent messages, not the percentage of all meaning or success carried by words, voice, and appearance. It is not a contemporary classroom effectiveness experiment. **Use:** coordinate tone and meaning without discounting words, evidence, or reasoning.

**P11 — Repetition can affect truth judgments.** Fazio, Brashier, Payne, and Marsh (2015), [Knowledge does not protect against illusory truth](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26301795/), DOI 10.1037/xge0000098. Original abstract inspected. Two experiments found that familiarity from repetition could influence truth judgments even when participants possessed relevant knowledge; modeling investigated reliance on fluency. The task does not show that repetition always overwhelms knowledge. **Use:** keep repeated cues linked to accurate evidence and varied retrieval. Never use familiarity as proof or as a persuasion target.

**P12 — Negative words and news clicks.** Robertson et al. (2023), [Negativity drives online news consumption](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4), DOI 10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4. Registered report; full text inspected. Analyses of randomized Upworthy headline variants found increased click-through with negative wording. The dataset covered approximately 105,000 variants and 370 million impressions; it measured clicking, not learning, subjective emotion, or religious motivation. **Use:** recognize the temptation to optimize attention through negativity while requiring proportionate framing and separate learning outcomes. This supplies no license to manufacture fear.

## Language, visuals, and processing

**P4 — Conversational language.** Mayer, Fennell, Farmer, and Campbell (2004), [A personalization effect in multimedia learning](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Mayer2004.pdf), DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.96.2.389. Original paper inspected. Three experiments compared conversational and formal wording in a short respiratory-system animation. Conversational wording improved transfer performance but not retention performance. This is a particular lesson and treatment, not proof that more second-person pronouns always improve memory. **Use:** natural direct address and everyday syntax without manufactured intimacy or a pronoun quota.

**P5 — Interesting but irrelevant detail.** Harp and Mayer (1998), [How seductive details do their damage](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Richard-Mayer-4/publication/232595492_How_Seductive_Details_Do_Their_Damage_A_Theory_of_Cognitive_Interest_in_Science_Learning/links/57799b8408ae4645d611f204/How-Seductive-Details-Do-Their-Damage-A-Theory-of-Cognitive-Interest-in-Science-Learning.pdf), DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414. Original author-posted paper inspected. Four experiments with illustrated science passages found poorer main-idea recall and transfer when interesting irrelevant details were added; manipulations investigated mechanisms. This does not prohibit art, humor, emotion, or relevant stories. **Use:** give details an explanatory purpose and check what learners retain from them.

**P6 — Seeing a diagram constructed.** Fiorella and Mayer (2016), [Effects of observing the instructor draw diagrams on learning from multimedia messages](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1099178), DOI 10.1037/edu0000065. Original author abstract in ERIC inspected. Four experiments used Doppler-effect lessons. Some visible drawing conditions improved transfer relative to completed diagrams; outcomes varied with prior knowledge and visibility of the hand/body. Delayed retention was not established by the inspected abstract. **Use:** consider narrated construction when it makes a relation traceable; do not animate by default or infer a benefit from motion alone.

**P7 — What instructor presence contributes.** Fiorella, Stull, Kuhlmann, and Mayer (2019), [Instructor presence in video lectures](https://ecrhub.org/publications/instructor-presence-video-lectures-role-dynamic-drawings-eye-contact-and-instructor), DOI 10.1037/edu0000325. Original abstract in the research-funder's publications record inspected. Two college-student experiments used kidney lessons, dynamic/static drawings, and differing whiteboard/presenter arrangements. Particular drawing and camera-eye-contact conditions improved posttest performance; merely showing the instructor did not establish an additional benefit. The arrangements bundle features and do not identify a universal cut cadence. **Use:** decide whether the face, hand, or evidence is helping the current task; retain full, accessible explanation.

**P8 — Faster speech and comprehension.** Jones, Berry, and Stevens (2007), [Synthesized speech intelligibility and persuasion: speech rate and non-native listeners](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/synthesized-speech-intelligibility-and-persuasion-speech-rate-and/), DOI 10.1016/j.csl.2007.03.001. Original author abstract in the institutional repository inspected. Experiment with 160 native/non-native listeners used synthesized banking descriptions at 155 or 178 words per minute, with/without background noise. Faster speech reduced comprehension and did not change persuasiveness. These rates and older TTS conditions do not define an ideal human narration speed. **Use:** adjust pace to understanding, language needs, density, and replay opportunities.

**P9 — Speech rate can alter argument processing.** Smith and Shaffer (1995), [Speed of speech and persuasion: Evidence for multiple effects](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/01461672952110006), DOI 10.1177/01461672952110006. Publisher's original abstract inspected. An experiment varied relevance, strong/weak arguments, and speech rate (180/220 words per minute). Faster speech reduced differential agreement with strong versus weak arguments; its persuasive effect depended on involvement. It does not establish an optimal learning speed. **Use:** keep enough processing time to evaluate reasons; do not treat speed-induced acceptance as successful teaching.

**P10 — Message headings and visual evidence.** Garner and Alley (2013), [How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension](https://www.writing.engr.psu.edu/ae_comprehension.pdf), International Journal of Engineering Education 29(6), 1564–1579. Original paper downloaded; abstract and paper text inspected. A comparison with 110 engineering students favored slides using a combined assertion/evidence and multimedia-principle treatment for comprehension, misconceptions, perceived load, and delayed recall. Multiple design features changed together; it cannot isolate a headline rule or guarantee transfer to Scripture. **Use:** concise message headings with visible supporting evidence, stable comparisons, and readable source context; choose other layouts when the learner's task requires them.

**P13 — Brain coupling is not emotional mind control.** Stephens, Silbert, and Hasson (2010), [Speaker–listener neural coupling underlies successful communication](https://hassonlab.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf3591/files/documents/pnas.1008662107.pdf), DOI 10.1073/pnas.1008662107. Original paper inspected. fMRI during natural storytelling/listening, including 11 listeners, related some temporal coupling measures to comprehension; unintelligible-language and mismatched-story conditions supplied comparisons. This did not measure a one-to-one transfer of emotion, single mirror neurons, dopamine release, or obedience. **Use:** qualified scientific context only; it does not justify a speaker's claim to control another mind.

## Evidence-to-craft boundary

The beat table, pause purposes, shot-selection table, rehearsal passes, argument checks, and proposed cross-format adaptations are design judgments. They are useful ways to operationalize goals; no cited experiment tested this whole package. Evaluate actual learner comprehension and delayed use before claiming efficacy. Preserve null results, task limits, and disagreement instead of converting a conditional finding into a universal presentation rule.
